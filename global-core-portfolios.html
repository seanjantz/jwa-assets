Reading the numbers

What each number actually tells you.

Four numbers do most of the work when you size up a portfolio. Here is how to read each one.

Annualized return
Since 1/1/2012
The smoothed yearly return that, if earned every year, would produce the same ending value as the actual year-by-year results. The cleanest single number for comparing long-term growth.
Worst calendar year
Worst year
The deepest single calendar-year decline in the period. Declines within a year may have been larger, but a shallower worst year means less recovery work and an easier time staying invested when markets get rough.
Yield
Trailing 12 months
The income from dividends and interest over the past twelve months, as a percentage of the portfolio's value. The cash-flow part of total return.
Fund expenses
Weighted expense ratio
The annual cost of the underlying ETFs, weighted by allocation. It comes out of fund returns automatically. Lower is better, and it compounds meaningfully over decades.
How they are built

Strategic, not reactive. Diversified, not diluted.

Every Global Core portfolio is built from the same five asset classes. What changes between tiers is the weighting, not the philosophy. Moving from Conservative to Moderate to Balanced to Growth is a question of how much movement you can live with, not which strategy you happen to land on.

01

Low-cost ETFs

Weighted fund expenses run between 0.05% and 0.10% a year. Every basis point saved on fees compounds for decades.

02

True diversification

Across asset classes (stocks, Treasury bills, gold) and geographies (U.S. and international developed markets). Not just a different shade of the same risk.

03

Built-in stability

Gold, Treasury bills, and dividend quality act as structural ballast. The cushion is engineered in, not bolted on.

04

Strategic, not tactical

Allocations are set with intent and held with discipline. We do not chase momentum, predict markets, or shuffle positions on news cycles.

05

Rebalanced annually

Drift is the enemy of risk discipline. Annual rebalancing keeps each portfolio anchored to its stated risk profile.

The five roles

Each holding plays a deliberate role.

The portfolios are not a collection of funds we happen to like. They are a system where every position answers a specific question about growth, income, or stability.

The compounding engine

Every dividend. Every interest payment. Automatically reinvested.

Dividends from SCHB, SCHD, and SCHF, along with interest from SGOV, are not pulled out as cash. They go straight back to work, buying more shares, generating more income, compounding quietly across decades.

This is not optional. It is the structural choice that turns a 2% yield into a meaningful share of long-term total return.

No cash sits idle, and the earliest dollars work the longest.

This is a projection, not a prediction. The range comes from 1,000 simulated paths that vary month to month around the expected return you enter, using each portfolio's historical volatility (about 6% a year for Conservative up to 12% for Growth). The middle outcome is the 50th percentile; half of the simulated paths finished above it and half below. The low and high outcomes are the 5th and 95th percentiles, so roughly 1 run in 20 finished below the low figure and 1 in 20 above the high. The default expected return for each portfolio is its hypothetical annualized return from January 2012 through September 2026, a period that included strong U.S. stock markets; your results will be different and may be lower. A note on customization

Models are a starting point, not a ceiling.

Many people sit comfortably inside one of the four Global Core portfolios. Others have preferences that deserve respect. A long-held position in a single stock you want to keep. A tilt toward a sector you believe in. A holding you want to avoid for personal reasons.

The framework bends. A custom allocation is built on the same five-role foundation, with the same discipline around cost, diversification, and rebalancing, then adjusted to your wishes.
